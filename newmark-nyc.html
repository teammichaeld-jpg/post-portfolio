The hard truth
None pencil as-is
Post has never owned in NYC. The model needs a tax exemption and rent growth; NYC rent stabilization blocks both.
In one sentence: Post buys 250 to 700 unit Sunbelt garden apartments, gets the property tax removed through a housing authority partnership, and grows rents to market. These are 1920s to 1960s New York elevator and walk-up buildings where 74% to 99% of the units are rent stabilized, taxes are a fifth of revenue, and rent growth is set by a city board. Trident is the only one with Post's scale and the only one worth a real look, and even that only at a price near a 7% cap.

Why it ranks second
It is the only deal with real free-market rent. 20 of 48 units (41%) are unregulated. That is the one place in these four packages where Post's "grow rents to market" playbook actually applies.

What the broker is selling
In-place rent is $1.17M a year. Legal rent is $1.73M. The pitch is $560K a year of upside, a 48% raise, "as apartments turn over."
35 of 44 units (78%) sit on preferential rents averaging $2,330 against a legal $3,592.
Two medical offices add $144K a year, 11% of revenue.
Why that upside is mostly fiction
Since the 2019 state law, a preferential rent stays with the tenant for as long as they stay. The landlord can only move to the legal rent when the unit is vacated.
Stabilized tenants in Queens paying $1,200 below legal rent do not leave. Turnover in buildings like this runs a few units a year. At that pace the $560K takes 15 to 20 years to show up.
Zero free-market units. There is nothing Post can reprice on day one.
Taxes are 21% of revenue and the city will reassess upward after a sale.
Underwrite this on in-place NOI with 2% to 3% annual increases set by the Rent Guidelines Board and nothing else. On that basis it is a 5.5% to 6% bond with a 1936 boiler.

Why it is not that simple. The Texas and Georgia structures Post uses (public facility corporations and housing authority partnerships) do not exist in New York. The closest New York tool is an Article XI exemption from the city's housing agency, which requires a nonprofit housing company to hold title, a regulatory agreement capping rents and incomes, and a City Council vote. It is used by preservation buyers, it takes a year or more, and it is usually partial. It is the one idea that would make these buildings look like a Post deal. It needs a New York affordable-housing lawyer's opinion before anyone underwrites it.

What to do with this

Before anything else
  • Ask the acquisitions lead one question: is New York in bounds at all? If the answer is no, send your friend a polite pass and spend the time on Sunbelt deals.
  • If yes, ask Newmark for pricing guidance on Trident as a whole and on the Astoria core alone, plus trailing 12 months and a DHCR rent registration for every building.
  • Underwrite Trident on in-place NOI only. Give the preferential-rent upside zero value in year one and a 3% to 5% annual turnover after that. If it does not clear a 7% yield on price on that basis, it does not work.
The big idea, if you want one
  • Post's real skill is not buying apartments. It is turning workforce housing into tax-exempt housing through public partnerships. New York has a version of that tool and almost no private buyers use it.
  • If a New York housing lawyer says an Article XI deal is doable on a rent-stabilized portfolio, the tax line on Trident alone is $3M a year. That is a new line of business, not one deal.
  • That is a six-month question, not a this-week one. Raise it as a question, not a recommendation.
